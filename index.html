Obana.Africa — a B2B commerce operating system

Product ownership across marketplace, vendor, sales partner & operations

Obana is usually described as a marketplace. It is closer to four products that have to agree with each other: a buyer-facing marketplace, a vendor platform, a sales partner platform, and the operational layer — orders, logistics, locations, sourcing — that keeps the first three honest.

The hard part is never the catalogue. It is that one order has to resolve into vendor fulfilment, a customer payment, a partner commission, a wallet balance and a logistics booking, and every one of those has to still be correct a month later when someone asks to withdraw.

Where it got technical

02

Wallets, commissions and withdrawals

Embedded finance inside a commerce platform

Vendors and sales partners both hold balances. Money arrives as a customer payment, is split by commission rules, lands in a wallet, and leaves as a withdrawal to a bank account. Each of those steps is a state, and states are where commerce products quietly lose money.

This is the work I would point a sceptical interviewer at. Not because the screens are complicated — they are not — but because the rules behind them have to survive retries, partial failures, duplicate events and a human being looking at a transaction history and deciding whether to trust us.

I defined the product strategy for the wallet systems and embedded finance, and the payment infrastructure was built from scratch rather than inherited — including integrating the Sales Partner App and the Embedly API.

Product questions that actually matter

When is a commission earned rather than merely expected? What is withdrawable versus pending? What happens to a balance when an order is cancelled after payout? Who is allowed to see, and who is allowed to move, money?

What that demands

Accuracy, auditability, reconciliation, permissions, idempotent handling of repeated events, clear failure states, and a transaction history a partner can read without needing support to explain it.

Theja / Tajiri — SaaS for businesses that sell in both worlds

Commerce SaaS within the Obana ecosystem · POS, storefront, inventory, locations

A merchant sells over a counter and online, and expects one stock figure. That single expectation is the whole product: point of sale, storefront, inventory, locations, orders and payments have to behave as one system or the merchant stops trusting any of it.

Calling this "a POS" undersells the problem. The POS is one surface onto a business-operations platform, and the interesting decisions are about synchronisation, location rules and what the merchant sees when two surfaces disagree.

Commercial path — both paths meet at the order

The order is the junction. It is also where most of the interesting product arguments happen: what a back order means to a vendor, when stock is committed, whether a sale is still a sale if delivery cannot be priced, and what the partner sees in their wallet the moment any of that changes.

Product decisions

Judgement, with the trade-off shown.

Features are easy to list. Decisions are harder, and more useful, because they show what I was willing to give up.

Problem
A merchant who had not finished configuring delivery could not take an order at all. Checkout refused it, and the business never heard that someone had tried to buy.
Options
Force setup before opening the store; block only the unpriced route; or accept the order and resolve delivery afterwards.
Decision
Accept the order, mark it as requiring delivery to be arranged, and leave it in front of the merchant to resolve.
Trade-off
Operational work moves to the merchant, and some orders need a phone call. In exchange, no sale is lost to configuration the buyer could not see and could not fix.
Outcome
Setting logistics up became something a merchant does to price delivery automatically, not a gate standing in front of their first sale.
Problem
Customers who already existed elsewhere in the business were being turned away at sign-in on a different platform, with an error that blamed their credentials.
Options
Create a second account under the same identity; refuse and send them to support; or recognise the account and offer the access it was missing.
Decision
Recognise the person, state which access their account does have, and offer to add the missing one in the same flow.
Trade-off
More logic at the identity layer, and a verification step to prove ownership. In exchange, one person is one account across the business.
Outcome
A dead end became a conversion path, and the support queue stopped receiving a problem that was never the user's fault.

About

How I ended up here.

I came into product through operations, which is the reason I think the way I do. When you have had to reconcile an order by hand, or explain to a vendor why their balance is wrong, you stop treating the back office as somebody else's problem and start treating it as the product.

That took me from supporting product operations into owning increasingly awkward systems: marketplaces with two sides and competing incentives, SaaS that has to behave identically over a counter and on a phone, and financial workflows where being approximately right is the same as being wrong.

Most of my work has been in African B2B commerce, which I think is an advantage rather than a footnote. The constraints here are real — fragmented supply, uneven logistics, payment friction, businesses digitising for the first time. You cannot design around them with a prettier screen. You have to understand the operation.

I work closely with engineers. I read the API, reproduce the bug, and bring a case rather than an opinion. I would rather delete a feature than ship one nobody asked for, and I would rather be told a constraint early than be protected from it.
