Obana.Africa

A B2B commerce platform connecting African retailers to verified suppliers — sourcing, vendor listings, orders, logistics and the money that moves between them.

The business runs on supply. A retailer needs to find a product they can actually get, from a vendor they can trust, at a quantity that makes sense, delivered somewhere real. So most of the product work is about making supply visible and orderable: vendors onboarding and listing their catalogue, products carrying variants and minimum order quantities, buyers discovering what is in stock, and custom sourcing for the things nobody has listed yet.

Once an order exists it has to move and it has to settle. Logistics, multiple locations and fulfilment on one side; on the other, balances vendors and sales partners can trust, commissions they can predict, and withdrawals that reach a real bank account. There was no payment infrastructure when I arrived — I defined the product strategy for the wallet systems and embedded finance, and we built it from scratch rather than bolting it on.

The commercial side had its own problem: it was losing deals to its own response time. Leads arrived and sat. I rebuilt how a lead is captured, routed and answered, so it reached a person while the buyer still cared.

  • Product discovery, categories and vendor visibility for buyers
  • Custom sourcing for products not yet on the platform
  • Orders, back orders, fulfilment, logistics and multiple locations
  • Product strategy for wallet systems and embedded finance
  • Payment infrastructure built from scratch; Sales Partner App and Embedly API
  • Lead capture, routing and response automation
  • QA across releases, working in GitHub, Vercel and Render

Thaja

The platform a business uses to open an online store and run a point of sale over the same stock. Built alongside Obana and connected to it.

A merchant sells over a counter and online, and expects one stock figure. That single expectation is the whole product: point of sale, storefront, inventory, locations, orders and payments have to behave as one system, or the merchant stops trusting any of it.

Calling it a POS undersells the problem. The till is one surface onto a business-operations platform, and the decisions that matter are about synchronisation, location rules, and what a merchant sees when two surfaces disagree.

  • Point of sale and online storefront over one inventory
  • Multi-location stock, with collection and delivery per location
  • Storefront creation, templates and custom domains
  • Payment connection and delivery pricing at checkout
  • The integration that lets an Obana partner run a Thaja shop

Calls

Two decisions I would make again.

Features are easy to list. Decisions are more useful, because they show what I was willing to give up.

Problem
A merchant who had not finished configuring delivery could not take an order at all. Checkout refused it, and the business never heard that someone had tried to buy.
Options
Force setup before the store opens; block only the unpriced route; or take the order and resolve delivery afterwards.
Call
Take the order, mark it as needing delivery arranged, and put it in front of the merchant to resolve.
Cost
Operational work moves to the merchant, and some orders need a phone call.
Why
No sale should be lost to configuration the buyer cannot see and cannot fix. Setting logistics up became a way to price delivery automatically, not a gate in front of a first sale.
Problem
People who already existed elsewhere in the business were turned away at sign-in on another platform, with an error blaming their credentials.
Options
Let them create a second account; refuse and send them to support; or recognise the account and offer the access it was missing.
Call
Recognise the person, say which access they do have, and offer to add the missing one in the same flow.
Cost
More logic at the identity layer, and a verification step to prove ownership.
Why
One person should be one account across the business. A dead end became a conversion path, and support stopped receiving a problem that was never the user's fault.
